# Readability directives

Last updated: 2026-10-21. Refresh when output structure, citation placement,
or audience requirements change.

Make every skill output understandable on its own. Lead with the takeaway,
use tables and bullets where they make information easier to scan, and put
required citations in an appendix at the end.

## Scope and shared standards

These directives govern readability for every delivered skill output,
including chat responses, reports, emails, internal notes, and exported files.

- Read this file from the working repository, including when a skill runs
  from a plugin cache. Do not use a cached or embedded policy snapshot.
- Use [the writing style guide](writing_style_guide.md) for voice and claims
  and [the naming directives](file_naming_directives.md) for delivered files.
  This file controls readability, layout, and citation placement where the
  shared guides overlap.
- Preserve required task content, agreed templates, milestone IDs, approval
  fields, and audience boundaries. Apply these directives within that
  structure; honor an explicit user-requested format.

## Lead with the key points

Open the output and each section with a concise summary of what matters.

- Start with the outcome, verdict, decision, or action the reader needs.
  Explain the evidence and consequences afterward.
- Use one sentence or a few short bullets for the section's key points.
  Do not add a separate summary heading to every section or repeat its content.
- Keep brief outputs brief. A short email or chat response does not need
  extra sections; its opening can carry the summary.
- Show material blockers, uncertainty, and required decisions where they
  affect the conclusion. State when a reviewed area has no material findings.

## Choose a readable structure

Prefer tables and bullet points over long prose when the information can be
compared, grouped, or acted on more easily that way.

| Information | Preferred presentation |
| --- | --- |
| Comparisons, findings, metrics, gaps, ownership, or status | Compact table with meaningful column labels |
| Key points, risks, questions, or actions | Short bullets; name the owner first for assigned actions |
| Ordered steps or dependencies | Numbered list |
| Context, reasoning, or a short email summary | Short paragraphs when connected prose is clearer |

- Keep one main idea per paragraph or bullet. Avoid dense cells, deep nesting,
  repeated qualifications, and tables so wide that readers must scroll to
  understand a finding. Split large tables by purpose when needed.
- Use clear headings and whitespace. Bold meaningful labels or deliverables
  sparingly. Do not rely on formatting alone to convey status or urgency.
- Scale detail to the decision and task complexity. Do not pad to a word
  count, copy source inventories, or turn an output into a data dump.

## Use plain, precise language

Write for the intended reader so they can understand the issue and act.

- Use familiar words, active voice, and direct sentences. Cut filler and
  needless repetition. Define necessary jargon once; explain acronyms on
  first use when the audience may not know them.
- Explain why each finding matters and the action or decision it requires.
  Include technical detail only when it supports understanding or execution.
- Preserve essential numbers, units, scope, and qualifications. Distinguish
  what a source states, what you infer, and what remains unknown. Never make
  uncertainty disappear to simplify the writing.

## Put citations in a final appendix

Keep the main narrative readable while making every material sourced claim
traceable. When citations are required, collect them in a final
**Appendix — Sources and evidence**.

- Put citation links, publication details, source section/page references,
  transcript timestamps, line numbers, and necessary short quotations in the
  appendix rather than throughout the narrative or inside findings tables.
- Map each appendix entry to the supported claim, finding, metric, or
  milestone by its descriptive name or existing ID. For external sources,
  include title, author/publisher, date when available, direct link, and the
  relevant finding. For project or meeting evidence, identify the file or
  meeting, version/date, precise location, and verification limits.
- Keep evidence collection and source checks rigorous. Moving citations does
  not permit unsupported claims, invented sources, or omitted limitations.
- Keep assumptions and caveats that change a decision in the main body;
  place detailed provenance in the appendix. Ordinary navigation links and
  links to the delivered artifact can remain where the reader needs them.
- For customer-facing drafts, preserve audience boundaries. If sources belong
  in separate internal notes, put the source appendix at the end of those
  notes, outside the copy-ready draft. Never expose private evidence merely
  to satisfy citation placement. Do not add an empty appendix when no
  citations are needed.
