# AI writing patterns

Use this guide to diagnose wording, not as a mechanical replacement list. Preserve facts, the requested register, and any applicable house style. Examples of specific details are illustrative only; do not invent equivalent details in a real rewrite.

## Content patterns

1. **Inflated significance, legacy, or broader-trend claims.** Cut claims that a routine fact is pivotal, enduring, a testament, a turning point, or evidence of a broader landscape. State the concrete consequence instead.
2. **Notability or media-coverage padding.** Do not list outlets, followers, or expert credentials unless they matter to the point and are sourced in the text.
3. **Superficial `-ing` tails.** Replace “highlighting,” “underscoring,” “reflecting,” “ensuring,” and similar phrase tails with a direct sentence, or remove them.
4. **Promotional language.** Remove “vibrant,” “groundbreaking,” “renowned,” “nestled,” “rich” in the figurative sense, and other ad copy. Use concrete description.
5. **Vague attribution.** Replace “experts say,” “industry reports,” and “observers note” with a named source already supplied, or cut the claim.
6. **Formulaic challenges or future-outlook sections.** Replace generic “despite challenges” language with the actual obstacle, action, or uncertainty.

## Language and grammar patterns

7. **High-frequency AI vocabulary.** Scrutinize words such as “additionally,” “delve,” “crucial,” “enhance,” “fostering,” “interplay,” “intricate,” “pivotal,” “showcase,” “tapestry,” “testament,” “underscore,” “valuable,” and “vibrant.” Replace only when a plainer word improves the sentence.
8. **Copula avoidance.** Prefer “is,” “are,” or “has” over inflated substitutes such as “serves as,” “stands as,” “marks,” “represents,” “boasts,” “features,” and “offers” when they mean the same thing.
9. **Negative parallelism and tailing negations.** Reduce “not only … but,” “it is not just …,” and clipped endings such as “no guessing.” Write the full claim plainly.
10. **Rule-of-three padding.** Do not force unrelated ideas into threes to sound comprehensive.
11. **Elegant variation.** Do not cycle through synonyms for the same subject merely to avoid repetition.
12. **False ranges.** Avoid “from X to Y” where the endpoints do not form a meaningful scale or progression.
13. **Passive voice and subjectless fragments.** Name the actor when doing so improves clarity. Convert fragments such as “No configuration file needed” into complete sentences when appropriate.

## Style patterns

14. **Em-dash overuse.** Replace unnecessary em dashes with periods, commas, parentheses, or a cleaner rewrite. Keep them where the author's voice or a style guide calls for them.
15. **Mechanical boldface.** Remove bold used only as visual emphasis. Preserve meaningful formatting.
16. **Inline-header vertical lists.** Collapse bold-label-plus-colon lists into prose or a genuine list when that is clearer.
17. **Title Case headings.** Prefer sentence case unless a house style requires title case.
18. **Decorative emojis.** Remove emojis that are merely presentation ornaments; keep them only when they fit the user's intended voice.
19. **Curly quotation marks.** Use straight quotes only when the document's style requires them; otherwise preserve the existing convention.

## Communication patterns

20. **Chatbot artifacts.** Remove “Great question,” “Of course,” “I hope this helps,” “Here is,” and similar assistant framing that became part of the content.
21. **Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers.** Remove generic claims about available information or training dates. Keep a real source limitation only when the text needs it and it is true.
22. **Sycophantic tone.** Replace excessive praise and agreement with a specific, proportionate response.

## Filler and hedging

23. **Filler phrases.** Tighten “in order to,” “due to the fact that,” “at this point in time,” “in the event that,” “has the ability to,” and “it is important to note.”
24. **Excessive hedging.** Remove stacked qualifiers such as “could potentially possibly” while retaining necessary uncertainty.
25. **Generic positive conclusions.** End on a concrete next step, limitation, fact, or implication instead of “the future looks bright.”
26. **Hyphenated-pair overuse.** Do not normalize every common compound modifier. House style and standard grammar win.
27. **Persuasive authority tropes.** Remove throat-clearing such as “the real question is,” “at its core,” “what really matters,” and “the heart of the matter” when the next sentence can stand alone.
28. **Signposting and announcements.** Remove “let's dive in,” “let's explore,” “here's what you need to know,” and similar narration of what the text is about to do.
29. **Fragmented headers.** Remove a generic one-sentence warm-up beneath a heading when it merely repeats the heading.

## Final checks

- Does the text make claims only the source supports?
- Does the rhythm sound natural rather than uniformly polished?
- Are the specifics useful rather than invented or decorative?
- Does the result match the writer's voice and the document's register?
- Have any slogans, filler, faux-depth phrases, or chatbot artifacts survived?
